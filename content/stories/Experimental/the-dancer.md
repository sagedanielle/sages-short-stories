---
title: "**The Dancer"
image: /images/the-dancer.jpg
---


Daddy never had much. He always said that one day he would get enough money to get us both plane tickets to the United States and start a restaurant there. I waited for that while washing myself with a bucket on the concrete in the back of our shed. It was warm enough, so we didn’t need a proper shelter aside from the rainy season. That season was nice, though, because I could wash in the rain. It would pour. Daddy had a little cart he would wheel around when I was young and sell roti to the tourists, but he wanted to sell more dishes. He always said he didn’t have enough for that, though. He said he was developing ways to do that more. And he did. Other men started coming around our little shed. Daddy had serious business meetings with them that I wasn’t allowed to go to. He sent me out onto the road during them. But one day he told me to stay home and sit there and get the men bottled water from the fridge, and I was shocked to find bottled water in our small, dirty fridge.

When I brought the water to them, an old man who smelled like the harbor told me to sit on his lap, and when I looked at Daddy he nodded. They talked in words I didn’t understand so well, and I felt sweaty and wished for my mom. She’d gone a long time ago and I didn’t know where she went. I looked out the cracks in our wall towards the road and imagined her figure through the slits. The man stood up and grabbed me to lift me off his lap. He looked in my eyes with grey cracked teeth and said I would make it big one day. He left then, but that memory stuck in my small brain. Daddy always said only kids with big brains go to school. 

When I got older the men came over more and would look at me a lot. They paid Daddy and would take me into the woods with them. I never liked that. I didn’t want to go, but Daddy told me it was for the best. That if I could go into the woods with them then we would get to go to the United States and he could start his restaurant and I could live the American dream with him. He said we had to suffer a little bit then to be happy later. I believed him. I went off with them and did what they told me to do just like Daddy said. I liked being a good girl for him.
He hugged me one day and spun me around in the air with a huge smile on his bristly face. I never saw him happy like that. He told me I had helped him get enough money to move to the city! We had enough to get a proper home and a shower and a toilet. He drank a lot that night and he told me he loved me with slurred words that made my skin prickle for some reason he said was not important and he took me to the woods that night. I wondered while we were in the woods why we didn’t just stay in the shack\.

We moved to the city the next week. A man picked us up on a motorbike and I sat in between the driver and Daddy while Daddy whooped and hollered on the highway towards the city. Instead of going to our new home like he said, he said we needed to make a quick stop by the new place I would work to help us get to the United States. 

I imagined living in the heaven daddy told me about. I wore beautiful gowns and went to elegant parties. I had the carefree cheeriness of the tourists, instead of my sinking feeling that I was never good enough.

I felt like I failed daddy when I danced, and he scoffed from the crowd. I knew it was my fault we didn’t have the money to move somewhere better. In the city, we lived under a cement outcropping with three families. Their daughters were all like me, but I never got along with them. When men came by, we all fought over who would go with them. Because we all knew it would be any money that could set us apart and get out of that life.

It felt good when I made those people feel good. I liked their faces from the audience sometimes, but other times their eyes looked at me like I wasn’t human, but rather a foreign object sent from some layer of hell. Other eyes pitied me. So I stopped looking at the crowd, because none of them could know me anyway. Daddy knew me, and he knew that I wasn’t pretty enough to get us to America. He knew I would never be good enough to live with four walls around us. He resented me, I saw it in his eyes. But he also needed me, and I saw that in his eyes too.

I hated the city. I hated how much attention I got. I felt like throwing up when men touched me and grabbed me, but daddy said that was right. I wondered what was so wrong with me that I felt so wrong about it. I decided my emotions weren’t right, so I stopped feeling.

I got dressed in the room I shared with all the daughters, but that day I stopped seeing anything real in the mirror or around me. I just saw shapes and demands. I did what I was told because my thoughts weren’t right, anyway. They all knew far better than me. Daddy also stopped talking about America. He stopped talking much at all. He left for long periods and I starved until I did what I knew how, and found a new man. His clever plans got us heaps of money, and he took me out to nice restaurants. I felt a little more when he was around. But when daddy got back he disappeared and I missed him with my emotions disappearing out into the open air.

When I danced, none of the eyes in the audience looked like his. I looked for them, I wanted to feel how I felt when he was around, but all their eyes looked at me from layers back. None of them saw me like how he saw me. Daddy looked at me like that too, and I never noticed it before. I stopped thinking much. Thinking and feeling never got me far. They just made me feel bad.

Some women who walked by me in the crowded streets had cloth over their hair and gowns that went all the way past their shoes. I tried not to stare, but I didn’t understand them. I couldn’t even see them. They walked by me with the same presence as a ghost with their gowns trailing behind them. Their eyes never met mine, but when they did it looked like a raw disgust that made me hope the rest of their face was different.

Women walked by me with light skin and spoke foreign words so joyously I wished I could understand them. I walked by them as a ghost, wishing I could inhabit their bodies for just a day. Just to see how it felt. I wondered if their daddy was proud of them. I wondered how they grew up, and if it was anything like mine. I thought they probably lived like the beautiful light skinned women on the billboards. On perfect beaches with perfect men. I wondered if people looked at them like they looked at me, but I thought not because I wasn’t as pretty as that. I was revolting to some, and that’s why I could never get daddy to his dream life.

I stopped bleeding one month, and I wondered why. My belly grew bigger, and I stopped eating altogether because I couldn’t perform if I got fat. No one would come. But I kept getting bigger, and a woman I worked with told me I was pregnant. I cursed god. How could he have gotten me pregnant?

Any child that came out of me would be cursed, just like me. A failure, and I could not bring a failure into the world. I asked the woman one night, with my stomach churning, how I could get rid of it. I couldn’t hide it much from the crowd, and what would we do if I couldn’t perform? We would starve. I stopped thinking and feeling again for a while until the situation was taken care of. I went back to dancing.

It all felt different then. I hated the audience. I hated the people who ran it. I hated the women I danced with. And my only love had deserted me. And I felt so hard and thought so much that I unraveled and I haven’t been anywhere since.
